# Introduction

## 1. HBB and Molecular Variant Interpretation
The human beta-globin gene (*HBB*), located within the beta-globin cluster on chromosome 11 (11p15.4), encodes the 146-amino-acid beta-globin subunit ($\beta$-globin). In adults, two beta-globin and two alpha-globin polypeptides assemble with four heme groups into tetrameric adult hemoglobin ($HbA$, $\alpha_2\beta_2$), the primary erythrocyte oxygen carrier (Thein, 2013). Inherited sequence variants in *HBB* underlie the most prevalent monogenic disorders worldwide, causing diverse hemoglobinopathies that include the $\beta$-thalassemias and structural variants such as sickle cell hemoglobin ($HbS$) (Weatherall, 2010; Taher et al., 2018). The molecular pathology of *HBB* exhibits marked allelic heterogeneity: over 300 distinct pathogenic variants have been documented, encompassing single-nucleotide substitutions, small indels, canonical splice disruptions, and regulatory alterations across the three exons, two introns, and untranslated regions (Thein, 2013; Giardine et al., 2007). Precise molecular characterization of *HBB* variants is therefore fundamental to genomic research and sequence-based variant classification.

## 2. Standardized Variant Representation
Systematic variant interpretation depends on standardized, unambiguous nomenclature. The Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) guidelines provide the international consensus framework for describing sequence variants across DNA, RNA, and protein levels (den Dunnen et al., 2016). Modern HGVS recommendations require sequence alterations to be anchored to a defined, versioned reference sequence, distinguishing coding DNA ($c.$) from predicted protein consequences ($p.$) and genomic coordinates ($g.$). In hemoglobin genetics, standardized representation is critical due to persistent legacy practices: beta-globin alterations were historically numbered from the mature polypeptide amino-terminal valine—omitting the initiator methionine—yielding widely recognized designations such as Glu6Val for sickle cell hemoglobin or Glu26Lys for hemoglobin E (Thein, 2013). Under modern HGVS standards, which designate the initiator methionine as codon 1, these variants correspond respectively to p.Glu7Val ($c.20A>T$) and p.Glu27Lys ($c.79G>A$) (den Dunnen et al., 2016). Resolving such legacy offsets and ensuring consistent syntactic translation require rigorous computational adherence to standardized reference models.

Beyond nomenclature syntax, variant interpretation is inherently dependent on the underlying reference sequence model. Coordinates, exon boundaries, reading frames, and canonical splice junctions are defined strictly relative to a specific reference transcript (O'Leary et al., 2016). Genomic (e.g., GRCh38) and transcript cDNA systems differ in numbering, and variants in overlapping genes, alternative isoforms, or untranslated regions can yield conflicting consequence assignments if the transcript reference is unspecified. To harmonize clinical reporting across databases, the Matched Annotation from NCBI and EMBL-EBI (MANE) initiative established MANE Select transcripts (Morales et al., 2022). For the human *HBB* locus, RefSeq accession NM_000518.5 serves as the canonical MANE Select transcript, establishing an invariant reference framework for unambiguous sequence evaluation.

## 3. Deterministic Molecular Consequence Computation
In contemporary bioinformatics workflows, the initial step in evaluating a sequence variant is computational molecular consequence prediction. Tools such as the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) (McLaren et al., 2016) and ANNOVAR (Wang et al., 2010) map alterations onto transcripts to deterministically infer biochemical impact on coding sequences—categorizing variants into synonymous, missense, nonsense, frameshift, and canonical splice disruptions. This rule-based molecular consequence determination relies on deterministic computational logic: the universal genetic code, precise coordinate geometry, and codified boundary rules governing open reading frames and canonical dinucleotide splice junctions (GT at donor sites and AG at acceptor sites). Crucially, deterministic consequence calculation operates independently of statistical or machine-learning pathogenicity predictors (e.g., PolyPhen-2, SIFT, CADD), which estimate disease likelihood from conservation or structural features rather than computing direct sequence translation. In the present study, computational evidence summarization is architecturally separated from deterministic consequence determination, ensuring that algorithmic execution remains strictly rule-based and reproducible.

## 4. Distinguishing Molecular Consequence from Clinical Significance
A fundamental principle in clinical and computational genetics is the conceptual distinction between molecular consequence and clinical significance. As articulated in the joint consensus standards of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP), molecular consequence describes the intrinsic biochemical or structural impact of a variant on the transcript or protein product (Richards et al., 2015). In contrast, clinical significance (pathogenicity) represents an evaluative determination of whether that molecular alteration causes human disease in a patient (Richards et al., 2015; Rehm et al., 2015). While molecular consequence serves as an essential input into clinical interpretation—such as establishing predicted loss of function for ACMG criterion PVS1—molecular consequence alone does not determine clinical pathogenicity. For example, a frameshift insertion is biochemically categorized as a null variant regardless of whether the affected gene causes haploinsufficiency, while missense variants with identical consequence categories can range from completely benign population polymorphisms to severe disease-causing alleles. Maintaining strict operational separation between molecular consequence derivation and clinical classification is therefore paramount to avoid overclaiming computational diagnostic capability.

## 5. External Annotation Resources and ClinVar
To benchmark and evaluate computational variant analysis tools, researchers frequently turn to public knowledgebases, most prominently NCBI ClinVar. ClinVar serves as the central public archive aggregating human genetic variants, associated phenotypes, and clinical significance interpretations submitted by clinical laboratories, diagnostic testing facilities, and expert panels (Landrum et al., 2018). ClinVar records provide standardized HGVS syntax, molecular consequence terms, and submitter classifications graded across a four-star review status system. However, ClinVar entries represent aggregated submitter assertions and literature consensus rather than primary in vitro experimental data, and substantial discordance can exist among submitters regarding variant interpretations, transcript choices, and protein nomenclature conventions (Harrison et al., 2017). Furthermore, variant repositories may contain historical annotation artifacts, such as truncated protein representations for frameshift variants or omitted protein consequence strings for non-coding and splice-site variants (Landrum et al., 2018). Consequently, ClinVar consensus records serve as a valuable external comparison reference, but cannot be treated as error-free biological ground truth.

## 6. Motivation and Research Gap
Despite the existence of broad genome-scale variant annotation suites, dedicated locus-specific analysis of *HBB* presents unique computational requirements, including the management of persistent legacy numbering offsets, handling of historical truncated protein nomenclature in external repositories, and rigorous architectural separation between deterministic rule execution and auxiliary evidence retrieval. General-purpose variant annotators are frequently embedded within extensive monolithic pipelines, making it challenging to isolate core sequence-transformation logic for transparent, reproducible evaluation. Best-practice guidelines for computational benchmarking emphasize that bioinformatics pipelines should be evaluated under predefined, frozen protocols that explicitly isolate primary performance endpoints from secondary exploratory comparisons, document representation differences, and transparently disclose selection biases and unrepresented mutation classes (Krusche et al., 2019; Stodden et al., 2016). The present study addresses this need by focusing specifically on deterministic, reference-aware derivation of molecular consequences for *HBB* variants and evaluating concordance against a curated external annotation reference under a frozen, audited computational protocol.

## 7. Study Objective and Contribution
The objective of this study was to evaluate deterministic molecular consequence prediction for *HBB* variants against a curated ClinVar-derived external annotation reference using a predefined and reproducible computational protocol. We developed a dedicated, rule-based computational engine anchored strictly to RefSeq NM_000518.5 and executed an audited benchmark against a frozen primary cohort of 55 curated *HBB* variants spanning seven biological mutation classes. The primary evaluation endpoint was predefined as molecular consequence concordance between the runtime deterministic engine output and the curated external molecular annotation, with software regression baselines strictly isolated. Secondary analyses evaluated exact protein nomenclature concordance, documenting specific representation disparities such as truncated frameshifts. In addition, an isolated synthetic negative control cohort ($N = 18$) was evaluated to assess input-validation robustness. This study explicitly evaluates computational concordance with curated external molecular annotations; it does not evaluate clinical significance, ACMG pathogenicity classification, diagnostic accuracy, or patient clinical outcomes.

Crucially, during CP2.2 curation, admission to the proposed primary cohort required deterministic engine agreement (`comparison_status == "EXACT_AGREEMENT"`). The primary cohort was therefore not selected independently of engine agreement, creating incorporation or verification bias. This selection mechanism limits interpretation of the observed concordance and prevents treating it as an unbiased estimate of performance on unseen *HBB* variants. Furthermore, while the primary cohort represents seven biological classes, four classes (C05 in-frame indels, C07 in-frame duplications, C08 splice donor disruptions, and C10 5' UTR regulatory variants) had zero primary candidates and were not evaluated in this benchmark.
